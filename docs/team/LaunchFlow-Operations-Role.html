Role brief Operations · Grays, Essex
The job

Running the day, so the building can carry on

LaunchFlow builds and looks after websites and business software for small firms around Thurrock. There is one person in it. This role is everything that happens daily — support, the builds, the posting, checking things actually work — so that person can keep building and selling instead of firefighting.

You would not be filling a gap in a big team. You would be the second person, and the first one who is not Shoji.

What the business actually is

Around twenty clients: taxi firms, a tuition centre, accountants, a window company, a beauty salon, a rendering firm. Mostly Thurrock, mostly owner-run, mostly not technical. They pay monthly for their website to be looked after and, on the bigger plans, for someone to write and post for them and run their advertising.

There are three published packages. They build on each other.

PackageWhat the client getsWhat it asks of you
Presence The website looked after: hosting, updates, backups, uptime monitoring, a monthly report, and small changes on request. Keep it up, keep it current, and make the monthly report tell the truth. No posting on this package.
Standard All of Presence, plus a written content programme every month — social posts, articles for the site, and Google Business updates. You are the editor. Every post is drafted before you see it; your judgement decides whether it goes out, and whether it sounds like the client rather than like us.
Growth All of Standard, plus their advertising managed, a monthly call, same-day replies and WhatsApp access. The highest touch we offer. A Growth client should never have to chase us for anything — if they do, something has already gone wrong.

Most of our clients are not on one of those three

Shoji has been building websites for fifteen years, and a lot of these clients came with him. Some have been paying the same amount since before these packages existed. Several are friends of twenty years. They are on personalised arrangements, agreed one at a time, that do not map neatly onto the table above — and that is deliberate, not untidy. It is how a small firm keeps clients for a decade.

You will meet this on your first day, so it is worth being straight about it now.

What a client pays never decides what work you do. LaunchOS has a switch per client for each service — social, articles, Google Business, advertising. The switch is the authority, not the invoice. If social is switched on, we post for them, whatever they pay. If it is off, we do not, even on the largest package. Shoji sets those switches; your job is to read them and work to them.

You will be able to see what people pay, and it is not your subject. Billing sits alongside everything else in the system, so figures will cross your screen. Never quote a price, never compare two clients, and never reassure a client about what they or anyone else pays — not even kindly. Anything about money goes to Shoji, every time. LaunchFlow UK Limited is not VAT registered, so if a client asks about VAT, that one fact is the whole answer.

Everything runs through LaunchOS, software built in-house for exactly this business. It is not a CRM somebody bought. Clients log into their own portal in it; you work the other side of the same system.

There are ten AI agents already working here

They draft the monthly reports, triage incoming support, write the social posts, qualify leads, write the morning brief, and watch the ad accounts. They draft; a person decides. Nothing an agent produces reaches a client until somebody approves it — and clearing that queue properly would be a real part of your day.

So this is not a job where you write everything from scratch. It is closer to being an editor with authority: the first draft usually exists, and your job is to judge whether it is good enough to send, fix it when it is not, and notice when the machine is wrong.

Two things worth knowing before you start

Where an article actually goes

Some of our clients’ websites are WordPress; most are custom applications we built. When you approve an article, LaunchOS handles the difference for you — it writes the post straight into WordPress where that is what the site is, and for everything else it serves the post to the application, which fetches and publishes it. You do not need to know which is which, and there is no separate process to remember.

What this means in practice: a post you approve appears on the client’s site at the minute it was scheduled for, on their own domain, at their own address. If it does not, that is a fault worth reporting rather than a thing to work around.

An approval in the queue is always real work

The system used to queue a monthly report for every client whether or not anything had happened that month, which meant the queue filled with documents about nothing and stopped being worth reading. It no longer does that: a month with nothing in it is recorded and left alone.

So treat the approvals queue as a real list. If something is sitting in it, somebody is waiting on it. Clearing it properly, rather than letting it grow, is one of the clearest signals of whether this job is being done well.

What is not finished, and you should know it

This is a young system that is still being built, and you would be joining it mid-build rather than after it settled. That is honest rather than alarming — but you should hear it from us now and not discover it in week two.

WhereThe state of it
Google Business posting Built and tested, not switched on — it needs an access approval from Google that is outside our control. Until it is live, the Google updates on the content packages are on hold and clients are not charged for work not done.
Social access Works, but only once a client has granted it. Right now almost none have, which is why getting that access is the hardest and most valuable part of this job.
Advertising The system watches ad accounts and reports on them, but no client has handed over an ad account yet. Growth is sold; the advertising side of it is still to be switched on client by client.
Everything else Support, tasks, projects, content, reports, billing and the client portal are live and in daily use.

Nobody expects you to fix this list. It is here so you can tell the difference between “this is broken and I should say something” and “this is not turned on yet and Shoji knows”. Knowing which is which, and saying so clearly, is genuinely most of what a good first hire does.

The hardest part of the job, honestly

To post for a client we need access to their Facebook Page, their Instagram and their Google listing. Almost none of them can do that themselves. They are plumbers, drivers and salon owners; they do not know what a Business Manager is and they should not have to.

So the method is: you request access from our side, then ring them and stay on the phone while they tap Approve. Not a PDF, not an email with eight steps. One notification, one blue button, you talking them through it. Every client page in LaunchOS shows exactly what is still missing and whose job it is, so you always know what to ask for.

Patience with non-technical people is the single most important quality in this role. More than writing, more than software. Someone who gets frustrated with a sixty-year-old taxi firm owner who cannot find his Facebook notifications will not be able to do this job, however good they are at everything else.

What we need you to be able to do

Required

  • Write plain English well. Client emails, social posts, articles. British spelling, no jargon, no waffle. This is most of the job.
  • Be organised without being chased. A list of forty tasks with dates on them is the tool; you have to actually work it.
  • Be patient on the phone with people who are not technical, and stay patient the third time.
  • Notice when something is wrong and say so, rather than assuming it is meant to be like that.
  • Comfortable in a browser all day — web apps, Facebook and Instagram, Google Business Profile, email.

Helpful, and teachable

  • WordPress, or any CMS.
  • Editing an image so it fits — cropping, resizing.
  • Having run a business page on Facebook or Instagram.
  • Basic SEO literacy — what a title tag is and why it matters.
  • Google Ads awareness, for the Growth clients.

None of the second list is a filter. All of it can be learned in a month by somebody with the first list.

You do not need to be able to code. Nothing in this role touches the codebase. If something needs building, it goes to Shoji.

How the job is measured

The system already counts all of this and writes it into the morning brief, so there is no separate reporting to do and no ambiguity about how you are doing:

  • Cases awaiting a first response, and any breaching their target.
  • Tasks open and overdue.
  • Approvals pending, and how stale the oldest is.
  • Content published, and failed.
  • Client reviews nobody has answered after five days.
  • Client satisfaction scores, where clients have rated a case.
  • Hours clocked, and who is clocked in.

Which means the honest version of the deal: nobody will be standing over you, and nothing you leave undone will go unnoticed. Those two facts are the same fact.

The rules that do not bend

  1. Nothing goes to a client without approval — yours once you have it, Shoji’s until then. No exceptions for "it was obviously fine".
  2. Never promise a date Shoji has not agreed. "I will find out today" is always available and always better than a guess.
  3. Never quote a price. Prices are on the website; anything else goes to Shoji. This is not negotiable even when the client pushes.
  4. Never post about a client without their approval in the system. Not a verbal yes on the phone — the approval in LaunchOS.
  5. Deleting is a last resort, and it asks you to prove you mean it. Records can be removed where it makes sense — a website listed twice, a client who never signed — and the system will make you type the thing’s own address before it goes, then refuse entirely if anything still depends on it. Archive rather than delete whenever archiving would do.
  6. A client’s data is theirs. Their enquiries, their customers’ details. It does not leave the system and it does not go in a personal spreadsheet.
  7. If you break something, say so immediately. Everything is backed up and almost everything is reversible. A broken thing hidden for a day is the only version that becomes serious.
What good looks like

Three months in, a client rings about something going wrong and you have already spotted it, already fixed it, and already told them. Shoji finds out from the timeline.

That is the whole job, described in one sentence. Everything above is the scaffolding that makes it possible.
